Everything so far has been the discipline of saying no: no to sending metrics through a language model, no to paying token prices for arithmetic. Tonight is the other half of the same discipline. A router that can only say no is just a wall, and a wall does not theme a single support ticket.

Self-studyWhy conviction matters as much as caution2 min▶

A half-hearted yes is the most expensive answer of all. Part 1 · covers judgment work's home ground

Where language is the actual work

7 min live

Unstructured language, synthesis, generation. No writable rule exists and no table contains the answer. That is the home ground, and all three of Cadence's remaining workloads live squarely on it.

LiveThe fit test: no writable rule, no table with the answer4 min▶

Run each workload back through session 1's first question. Theming 500 tickets: can you write the rule that maps "app eats my notes" to the same theme as "data loss"? You can enumerate keywords for a week and a customer will paraphrase past your list on day eight. Drafting an onboarding email that reflects what this specific customer signed up to do: there is no template slot for that. Extracting "Sam said he'd send the pricing doc by Friday" from a transcript: no regex on earth, because the commitment is in the meaning, not the characters.

A genuine no on the triage question is a real license to spend. Not a shrug, a license. The same test that protected the deterministic tier from token prices now protects the judgment tier from keyword tooling. It is one test, honest in both directions.

The one-line fit test, written down. "The input is unstructured language, the output requires synthesis or generation, and no writable rule connects them." If all three clauses hold, the workload is on GenAI's home ground. If any clause fails, a cheaper rung wants the work back.

LiveWhy paying more here is the correct decision4 min▶

Watch what the simulator does when the GenAI fit lever comes on: the bill rises from $62 to $176, and the trusted counter goes from 7 out of 10 to 10 out of 10. Cost UP $114, and it is the best $114 in the whole course. Session 2's lesson was never "cheap is good". It was "pay for what the work needs and nothing else". These three workloads need judgment over language, and $114 a month is what judgment over language costs at Cadence's volume.

Compare the alternative honestly. The keyword tooling was nearly free and produced ticket themes nobody used, emails nobody opened, action items nobody caught. Free output that no one trusts has a price too - it is just paid in missed renewals and dropped commitments instead of invoices. Where language is the actual work, the tokens are the bargain.

Real world

Cadence's support lead spent two quarters extending the keyword list for ticket theming. Every month it caught last month's phrasings. The dashboard tier is magnificent at counting things - session 2 proved it - but a dashboard cannot know that "my notes vanished mid-call" and "sync ate my meeting" are the same complaint. That knowing IS the work, and it is exactly the work language models were built for.

Part 2 · covers the cascade pattern

The cascade: cheapest capable tier first, escalate the residue

6 min live

Saying yes to GenAI does not mean saying yes to the biggest model for every call. Even on this branch the routing instinct holds: send each request to the cheapest tier that can handle it, and escalate only what that tier cannot. Two published cases show the size of the prize.

LiveCheckr: frontier to fine-tuned small model, roughly 5x cheaper3 min▶

Checkr runs background checks, and classifying messy records is genuine judgment-over-language work. As reported by Computerworld, they moved that classification off GPT-4 onto a fine-tuned Llama-3-8B: the bill went from roughly $12,000 a month to under $800 - about 5x cheaper - while latency dropped from around 15 seconds to 0.15-0.5 seconds, roughly 30x faster. Accuracy on their hardest, messiest record class went from the 55-72 percent range to 85-90 percent or better.

Two honesty notes travel with this case. First, it is a small-model-replaces-frontier story, not a regex-replaces-LLM story - the work stayed on the GenAI branch, it just moved to a cheaper capable tier. Second, the fine-tuning vendor was involved in telling it, so the numbers here are the Computerworld-reported version, and a healthy reader holds vendor-adjacent case studies a notch looser than audited figures.

LiveGoogle Cloud: proxy models, over 100x on per-row LLM calls3 min▶

Google Cloud published the same pattern at a different scale: analytical SQL pipelines that called an LLM per row were rebuilt so that lightweight proxy models handle the majority of rows, and only the rows the proxy is uncertain about escalate to the full LLM. Reported result: more than 100x reduction in cost and latency for the pipeline as a whole.

Notice what both cases have in common. Neither one abandoned GenAI - the frontier model is still in the loop, doing the residue that genuinely needs it. The saving came from refusing to pay frontier prices for the easy majority. That is the routing instinct from sessions 1 through 3 applied INSIDE the GenAI branch, and it is why the cascade belongs in tonight's session and not in a cost-cutting appendix.

The cascade in one sentence. Route every request to the cheapest tier that can handle it, define what "cannot handle it" looks like before you ship, and let only that residue climb. The published range for the prize is 5x to over 100x.

Part 3 · covers the boundary of the win

Where LLMs quietly fail: the wrong work

5 min live

Full conviction on the home ground requires equal clarity about the border. Language models fail in a specific, quiet way when handed deterministic work, and one famous benchmark trap makes them look better on tables than they are.

LiveFluent when wrong: the $11k that read perfectly3 min▶

The simulator's baseline configuration shows the failure in its natural habitat: a monthly revenue total answered by a language model comes back in a well-formed sentence, confidently wrong by $11,000, and nothing about the output signals it. It surfaced only at month-end reconciliation - which is to say, it was caught by the deterministic tier doing its job weeks late. Seven of the baseline's ten answers are silently wrong like this, which is what makes its true cost $2,315 while its API bill is $5.

This is not a capability gap that the next model generation fixes, because the problem is structural: a checkable answer delivered through an unverifiable channel is unverifiable by construction. The words carry no audit trail. On judgment work that is fine - a draft email has a human reader as its verification step. On a revenue number, the fluency is camouflage.

Self-studyThe contamination trap: acing a famous CSV proves memorization4 min▶

One more border marker, from the tabular evidence stack session 3 introduced. Bordt, Nori, Caruana and colleagues showed at COLM 2024 that large language models have memorized popular tabular datasets verbatim - the famous CSVs that circulate in every tutorial were in the training data, row by row.

The consequence for a router: an LLM acing a well-known benchmark table is evidence of memorization, not of tabular capability. If a demo shows a language model predicting beautifully on a dataset you recognize from a textbook, the demo has shown you a recall test. Session 3's evidence stack stands: on typical structured prediction, trained tabular models remain the tool of record, and the honest way to evaluate any model on YOUR table is data the model has never seen.

Real world

A vendor pitched Cadence's team with an LLM demo scoring near-perfectly on a churn dataset the data lead recognized within a minute - it was a public teaching dataset with its own Wikipedia-adjacent fame. On Cadence's own churn table, the same setup performed no better than the classic model from session 3, at many times the cost per prediction. The demo was not dishonest. It was contaminated, which from a budget's point of view is the same thing.

Build-along

Design the cascade, on paper

★ 18 min · everyone builds

Three judgment workloads, one cascade design each. No code tonight - the design decisions are the hard part, and they fit on a page. Session 5 prices exactly what you write down now.

Write the fit test line for each workload. One sentence per workload: is the input unstructured language or judgment? Does no writable rule exist? For ticket theming, email drafting, and action-item extraction the honest answer is yes and yes - but write the sentence anyway, because the sentence is what you defend in the budget meeting.

Pick the cheapest starting tier for each. Template, small model, or frontier? Action-item extraction is well-scoped per transcript - start small. Ticket theming needs consistent taxonomy over messy paraphrase - small model with a clear theme list, escalate the weird ones. Personalized onboarding emails carry Cadence's voice to a brand-new customer - that draft may earn a frontier tier, and writing WHY is the exercise.

Define the escalation trigger before you ship anything. What does "this tier cannot handle it" look like, concretely? Low confidence on theme assignment, a transcript over a length threshold, an email for an unusual plan type. If you cannot describe the trigger, you have not designed a cascade - you have just picked a model.

Set the human gate where the blast radius demands one. Onboarding emails leave the building with Cadence's name on them: a human approves every send. Ticket themes feed an internal dashboard: spot-check weekly, no gate per item. Action items go to account owners who will notice a bad one: light review. The gate is sized to the cost of a miss, not to a general anxiety.

Sanity-check the whole design against the lever. Your three cascades together are what the simulator prices at $176 a month against $62 without them - $114 for three workloads moving from failing to trusted. Write the one-line justification under your design. If it does not convince you, it will not convince finance.

Bring the designs to session 5. Tonight you chose tiers, triggers, and gates. Session 5 puts real per-token prices on every choice you just made - and adds the layers of cost that never appear on the API invoice, which is where most GenAI budgets quietly go wrong.
